01The Transformer Architecture

The Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) replaced recurrence with self-attention, processing all positions in parallel. This single architectural change enabled modern LLMs, from BERT to GPT-4.

⚡ Parallel processing

Unlike RNNs that process tokens one by one, the Transformer attends to all positions simultaneously. Training time drops from O(n) sequential steps to O(1).

◎ Direct connections

Self-attention connects every token to every other token in a single step. No vanishing gradients — the path length between any two tokens is O(1).

▦ Key dimensions

d_model=512 h=8 heads d_k=d_v=64 d_ff=2048 N=6 layers

02Embeddings & Positional Encoding

Tokens become dense vectors, then positional information is added. Without position encoding, the Transformer can't distinguish "dog bites man" from "man bites dog."

▤ Step 1: Token embedding

Each token maps to a learned vector of dimension d_model=512. We use d_model=4 here for visibility. Embeddings are scaled by √d_model (Section 3.4).

∿ Step 2: Sinusoidal positional encoding

Position information uses sine/cosine waves at different frequencies — each dimension gets a unique pattern. Low dimensions oscillate fast (fine position), high dimensions oscillate slowly (coarse position).

PE(pos, 2i)   = sin(pos / 100002i/d_model)
PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 100002i/d_model)
Positions are counted from 0 throughout this guide (pos = 0 for the first token), as in the authors' reference code (tensor2tensor) and the Annotated Transformer. The first token therefore gets PE(0) = [0, 1, 0, 1, …].
Why sinusoidal?
For any offset k, PE(pos+k) is a linear function of PE(pos). This lets the model learn relative positions. Wavelengths form a geometric progression from 2π to 10000·2π.

03The Intuition Behind Self-Attention

Before the math: what does self-attention actually do to a sentence? Three ideas carry the whole section. Every word reads the other words. Reading is directional — how much it reads from animal is a different number from how much animal reads from it. And what a word ends up with is a weighted mix of the other words' values, with the weights coming out of a softmax.

◉ 1 · The problem: a word on its own does not know enough
"The tired animal didn't cross the wide street because it was afraid of the cars."

The embedding of "it" is the same vector in every sentence ever written. On its own it cannot tell you whether it means the animal or the street. To be useful at this position it has to go and get that information from the rest of the sentence — and it has to decide where to get it from: mostly from animal, a little from street and cars, hardly anything from because. The two adjectives have the same problem in miniature: tired belongs to animal, wide belongs to street, and each has to find its own noun.

Notice that the need is one-sided. it badly needs animal. animal does not need it at all — it already knows what it is. Whatever mechanism we build should let the traffic run mostly from animal to it, not equally in both directions.

Key insight
Self-attention gives each word its own set of weights over all words — a row of numbers saying how much that word reads from each other word — and then builds the word's new vector as the weighted mix. Different words, different rows. That is what makes it directional.
⊞ 2 · Every word plays two roles: reader and read

Each word is turned into three vectors by three learned matrices WQ, WK, WV. Two of them belong to the word as something that is read; one belongs to it as a reader.

🔍
Query qi — the word as a reader
"What am I looking for?" For it: a noun, preferably a living one, somewhere near me.
🏷️
Key kj — the word as something to be found
"This is what I can offer." For animal: I am a noun, I am animate, I am at position 2.
📖
Value vj — the word as something to be copied
"This is what you get if you read me." For animal: the content that will actually flow into it.
Why the direction is built in
A pair of words (i, j) is scored with qi·kj — the query of the reader against the key of the one being read. Swap the roles and you get qj·ki, a completely different product, because WQ and WK are different matrices. "Does it want animal?" and "does animal want it?" are two different questions, and they get two different answers.
≡ 3 · Three steps, for one reader i
Score every word
scoreij = qi·kj / √dk
One number per other word j, positive or negative. This is the only place Q and K appear.
Softmax the row
Aij = softmaxj(scorei·)
Turns the row of scores into attention weights: all ≥ 0, summing to 1. These weights are "the attention". They do not touch V.
Mix the values
zi = Σj Aij · vj
Only now does V enter: the weights multiply the value vectors and the sum is the new vector for word i.
A = softmax( Q·KT / √d_k ) // n×n, row i = how reader i spreads its attention
Z = A · V // n×d_v, row i = what reader i ends up holding
Read the index the right way round
A[i][j] = reader i (query) attends to word j (key). Row i belongs to reader i and sums to 1. A[j][i] lives in a different row, is computed from a different query and key, and is in general a different number. Columns sum to nothing in particular.

What is faithful here and what is not
Faithful to the paper: the three steps, the √dk scaling, the softmax, no mask, every row summing to 1, and the picture (the layout of Figure 3 in the appendix). Not faithful: the numbers. The paper reads attention out of a trained encoder with dmodel=512 and 8 heads. Here each word is a 15-dimensional toy vector (8 part-of-speech features, one "animate" feature, and six sinusoidal position features, so that similarity decays with distance the way the paper's positional encoding does), dk=8, dv=4, and WQ, WK, WV were set by hand so that a pronoun asks "which noun, preferably a living one?", an adjective asks "which noun is next to me?", a noun answers "me", and values carry four readable features. Words the head cannot link in one step — afraid back to animal — are left for other heads and layers, as in the real model. Nothing was trained.
△ 5 · Same word, new vector

Nothing about the word it changed. What changed is the vector standing at its position: before attention it was the value of a pronoun — empty — and after one head it is mostly the value of animal, with a little street and cars mixed in. The next layer, and eventually the output, works with this new vector. That is what "contextualised representation" means, and it is why bank next to river and bank next to account end up as different vectors even though they start as the same one.

04Scaled Dot-Product Attention

Now the math. Each token gets a Query, a Key and a Value vector. The score of reader i for token j is qi·kj, scaled by √dk; a softmax over each row turns the scores into attention weights; the weights then multiply V. Every number below is computed, and the example is deliberately not symmetric.

≡ The attention formula (Equation 1)
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKT / √d_k ) · V

Five steps: (1) Project — Q = XWQ, K = XWK, V = XWV. (2) Score — QKT; cell (i, j) = qi·kj, reader i against token j. (3) Scale — divide by √dk to keep softmax out of its flat region. (4) Normalize — softmax per row, so each row sums to 1; these are the attention weights. (5) Aggregate — weights × V, a weighted sum of the value vectors.

⊘ Why divide by √d_k?

When d_k is large, dot products grow in magnitude, pushing softmax into near-zero gradient regions. Scaling by √d_k keeps variance ≈ 1 regardless of dimension.

WITHOUT scaling (softmax saturates)
WITH √d_k scaling (gradients flow)
The gradient problem
When softmax outputs approach 0 or 1, its gradient vanishes — the model can't learn. Scaling prevents this by keeping scores in a moderate range where softmax is sensitive to input changes.

05Multi-Head Attention

One head can only ask one kind of question per token — "which noun is near me?" is a different question from "which word comes before me?", and one softmax cannot answer both at once. So the paper runs h = 8 heads in parallel, each with its own small WQ, WK, WV, and glues their answers together.

⧉ The formula (Section 3.2.2)
MultiHead(Q, K, V) = Concat(head₁, ..., headₕ) · WO

where headi = Attention(X·WᵢQ, X·WᵢK, X·WᵢV)

// Wᵢ^Q, Wᵢ^K: d_model × d_k Wᵢ^V: d_model × d_v W^O: h·d_v × d_model
// paper: h = 8, d_k = d_v = d_model / h = 512 / 8 = 64
Where the split really happens
A head does not get a slice of the input. Every head reads the whole 512-dimensional token vector and projects it into its own 64-dimensional space with its own three matrices. The slicing is on the output side: the h results, 64 numbers each, are laid side by side into 512 numbers, and WO mixes them back into one vector.
▤ The shape of it
One token through multi-head attention — full vector in, h small projections, concat, WO, full vector out
Cost
h heads of size dk = dmodel/h have exactly as many parameters as one head of size dmodel: 4·dmodel² for WQ, WK, WV, WO together, whatever h is. The diversity of attention patterns is free.

◉ What trained heads turn out to do

Nobody tells a head what to attend to; the specialisation emerges from training. Analyses of trained Transformers (e.g. Clark et al. 2019, Voita et al. 2019) find recurring types:

Positional heads
Attend to the previous or next token, or to the token itself. Your head 2 above is one of these. Very common in the lower layers.
Syntactic heads
Follow a dependency: verb → its subject, preposition → its object, adjective → its noun. Your head 1 is a toy version.
Rare-token / delimiter heads
Park attention on punctuation, [SEP] or <eos> when there is nothing useful to read — a learned "no-op". Many heads can be pruned with little loss.
Why several small heads beat one big one
A single head with dk = 512 still produces one softmax row per token — one weighting of the other words. Eight heads produce eight weightings, each in its own 64-dimensional subspace where a different similarity can be measured; WO then decides how much of each answer the token keeps.

06The Encoder Block

One encoder layer is attention plus a small per-token network, each wrapped in the same two-line habit: add the input back (residual), then normalise (LayerNorm). Below, the three tokens from Section 04 are pushed all the way through one layer, every number computed.

↻ The wrapper: Add & Norm

Every sub-layer — attention or feed-forward — is used as a correction to its input, not a replacement: x + SubLayer(x). Then each token's vector is normalised on its own, across its dmodel numbers.

output = LayerNorm( x + SubLayer(x) )

// LayerNorm works on ONE token vector at a time, across its d_model entries:
μ = mean(x), σ² = var(x)
LayerNorm(x) = γ · (x − μ) / √(σ² + ε) + β // γ, β learned, one pair per dimension
Why add x back
If a sub-layer has nothing useful to say, it can output ≈ 0 and the token passes through unchanged. With N=6 layers stacked, this identity path is what lets gradients reach the first layer and lets the network start from "do nothing" and learn corrections.
Why normalise per token
After several additions the vectors drift in scale. LayerNorm re-centres each token to mean 0 and variance 1 before the next sub-layer sees it — no dependence on the batch, no dependence on other positions. Contrast with BatchNorm, which normalises across examples.
▦ Position-wise feed-forward network (Equation 2)

Attention moved information between tokens; the FFN now processes each token on its own. It is the same two-layer network at every position — expand to dff, ReLU, contract back — and it holds about two thirds of the layer's parameters.

FFN(x) = max(0, x·W₁ + b₁) · W₂ + b₂

// W₁: 512 × 2048 (expand) → ReLU → W₂: 2048 × 512 (contract)
FFN shape — expand then contract
The ReLU is the point
Two linear layers in a row would collapse into one. The ReLU in between is the only place in the whole encoder layer where a non-linearity acts on a token's own content — attention is linear in V once the weights are fixed. The wide inner layer gives the ReLU 2048 different "features" to switch on or off per token.

07The Decoder & Masking

The decoder adds three things to the encoder block: (1) masked self-attention, so that position i can only read positions ≤ i; (2) cross-attention, which reads the encoder's output; and (3) the output head, a linear layer plus softmax over the vocabulary on the last layer (Section 08). The numbers below reuse the three-token example.

◧ Masked self-attention — the same three tokens, with the future hidden

The decoder is trained to predict the next token, so if position 0 could read position 1 it would simply copy the answer. The mask adds −∞ to every score above the diagonal before the softmax. Left: the scores from Section 04. Right: what the softmax does with them, unmasked (encoder) and masked (decoder).

Why −∞ and not 0?
softmax(−∞) = e−∞ = 0 exactly, whatever the other scores are. A score of 0 would still get e0 = 1 unit of weight. In code the mask is a large negative number like −10⁹, which is the same thing after exp.
What changes and what does not
Row 0 becomes 100 % on itself — with nothing to its left it has nothing else to read. Row 1 loses tok₂, and the two remaining weights are renormalised so they still sum to 1. Row 2 is untouched: the last position can see everything. The mask is always on, also at inference — the decoder is causal by construction.
Mask added to the scores
Shape of the weights after softmax (rows sum to 1)
⇋ Cross-attention — the decoder reads the encoder

Same formula, different sources: the queries come from the decoder (what am I generating now?), the keys and values come from the encoder output (what does the source sentence contain?). Because the two sides have different lengths, the weight matrix is rectangular: one row per target position, one column per source token.

Q = decoder states × WQ // m target positions
K = encoder output × WK // n source tokens
V = encoder output × WV

A = softmax( Q KT / √d_k ) // m × n — no mask: the whole source is always visible
Where the encoder's work ends up
The encoder runs once; its final output is what every decoder layer reads through cross-attention. This is the only road from source to target — the gradient of the translation loss reaches the encoder only through these K and V.

08Training & Generation

Training and generation use the same network in two different ways: during training the decoder sees the whole target sentence at once and is scored at every position in parallel; at generation time it produces one token, feeds it back, and produces the next. The mask is what makes the first possible without cheating.

▶ Teacher forcing — the target appears twice, one position apart

The decoder input is the target shifted right by one (a start token in front); the decoder target is the same sentence with the end token behind. Position t reads inputs 0…t and is asked for target t — which is input t+1. Thanks to the mask, one forward pass of one training step produces the prediction and the loss for every position at once, instead of one position per pass as in an RNN. The model is still trained over many such steps (100 k for the base model).

// Translate "I love ML" → "Ich liebe ML"

Encoder input: [I] [love] [ML]
Decoder input: [<bos>] [Ich] [liebe] [ML] // what the decoder reads
Decoder target: [Ich] [liebe] [ML] [<eos>] // what it must predict, one step ahead
Exposure bias — the catch
In training the decoder always reads the correct previous words. At generation it reads its own previous words, mistakes included, and it has never practised recovering from them. The paper does not solve this; beam search and label smoothing soften it.
↝ Auto-regressive generation (inference)

Encoder once, decoder in a loop: predict → append → predict again, until <eos>. Each step re-runs the decoder on the whole prefix; the causal mask is still applied (it is part of the layer), and in practice the keys and values of earlier positions are cached rather than recomputed.

// Translate "I love ML" → "Ich liebe ML" — at inference

Encoder input: [I] [love] [ML] // run once, output kept
Decoder input: [<bos>] // step 1 → predicts Ich
[<bos>] [Ich] // step 2 → predicts liebe
[<bos>] [Ich] [liebe] // step 3 → predicts ML
[<bos>] [Ich] [liebe] [ML] // step 4 → predicts <eos> → stop
Decoder target: none // nothing to compare against; the last prediction is appended and fed back
Training vs. inference, same three lines
Encoder input: identical, and the encoder runs once in both cases. Decoder input: in training the whole shifted target at once; at inference the model's own output so far, one token longer each step. Decoder target: only exists in training — at inference there is nothing to score, only a next token to pick. The causal mask is applied in both.
Greedy vs. beam search
Taking the single most likely token at every step (greedy) can paint the sentence into a corner. The paper keeps the 4 best partial sentences at each step (beam size 4, length penalty α = 0.6) and returns the best complete one.
